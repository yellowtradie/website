# Late payment interest explained

> **Key facts**
>
> * Statutory interest is 8 percent a year above the Bank of England base rate for late business debts. Read on gov.uk on 24 October 2026.
> * Bank Rate was 4.00 percent on 24 October 2026 on bankofengland.co.uk. Statutory rate on that date was 12.00 percent a year.
> * Fixed recovery sums are £40 to £999.99 and £70 to £9,999.99 and £100 at £10,000 or more. Read on gov.uk on 24 October 2026.
> * Court interest where the Act does not apply is 8 percent under section 69 of the County Courts Act 1984. Read on gov.uk on 24 October 2026.

<p class="tertiary">By the YellowTradie team. Sources linked throughout.</p>

<p class="tertiary">24 October 2026 · 7 min read · Money</p>

Interest on a late invoice is how the law compensates you for waiting. It is not a fine to punish the customer. This post explains three things. The statutory rate at 8 percent above base rate. Your own fixed rate. What is different when the customer is not a business.

This is general information. It is not legal advice.

## The statutory rate

The rule for business to business debts is in the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998. The statutory rate is 8 percent a year above the Bank of England base rate. Read that on gov.uk on 24 October 2026.

You do not need to have written that rate into your terms to claim it where the Act applies. It is a right the Act gives. It sits alongside your contract.

The base rate is set by the Bank of England. It moves over time. When the Bank changes its rate the statutory rate for new periods of lateness moves with it. The rate you use is the one in force for that period.

On 24 October 2026 the Bank Rate was 4.00 percent on bankofengland.co.uk. Statutory interest on that date was therefore 12.00 percent a year.

## How the sum is worked

Interest is simple and daily. Daily rate equals annual rate divided by 365. Interest due equals daily rate times the amount still owed times days overdue.

Use the balance that is late. Not the original invoice total. If the customer has paid part of the bill work interest on what remains.

Example. Amount overdue £1,500. Days overdue 30. Annual statutory rate 12.00 percent.

Daily rate is 0.12 divided by 365. That is about 0.0329 percent a day.

Interest is 0.12 divided by 365 times £1,500 times 30. That is about £14.79.

At your own fixed rate of 8 percent the same 30 days on £1,500 is 0.08 divided by 365 times £1,500 times 30. That is about £9.86.

Common amounts at 12.00 percent a year on 24 October 2026 were:

| Amount overdue | 14 days | 30 days | 60 days |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| £500 | £2.30 | £4.93 | £9.86 |
| £1,000 | £4.60 | £9.86 | £19.73 |
| £1,500 | £6.90 | £14.79 | £29.59 |
| £2,500 | £11.51 | £24.66 | £49.32 |
| £5,000 | £23.01 | £49.32 | £98.63 |

At 8 percent fixed each figure is two thirds of the statutory figure shown. So £1,500 at 30 days is about £9.86 at 8 percent.

If only part of the invoice is overdue use that part in the sum. The calculator on this site shows every step.

## Debt recovery costs

The Act also lets you claim a fixed sum for chasing a business debt on top of interest. The fixed sum turns on the debt. It was read on gov.uk on 24 October 2026.

* Up to £999.99 debt is £40
* £1,000 to £9,999.99 debt is £70
* £10,000 and over debt is £100

You can claim only one fixed sum per debt when payment becomes late. You can claim reasonable costs beyond that fixed sum where the fixed sum does not cover your costs. You will need to show them.

You cannot add interest on top of interest. Do not include an interest figure inside a new invoice total until the customer has been told and given time to pay.

## Your own fixed rate

You can set your own interest rate in your written terms instead of relying on statutory. That contract rate applies where the customer agreed it.

The sum is worked the same way. Put your annual rate in place of the statutory rate. Daily rate is your annual rate divided by 365. Then times balance times days.

Example. Your terms say 8 percent a year. Balance £2,500 for 30 days is about £16.44. At 5 percent it is about £10.27 for the same 30 days.

A contract rate must be fair where the customer is a business. It must not create a penalty in English law. In practice keep it modest and bring it to the customer before the work starts. Put it on the invoice so they can see it.

If you name a very high fixed rate to deter late payment the customer can still challenge it. A court will look at whether the term was transparent and proportionate.

## The customer who is not a business

The law draws a line between two kinds of customer.

A business customer is another business. A consumer is a person acting outside their trade for personal use. A couple who hire you for their own home are consumers. A landlord who lets property as a business is a business customer for this purpose.

For a business customer statutory interest and the fixed sums are available where the Act applies. The right to statutory interest can sit alongside your own terms. You can rely on whichever gives the better result subject to any terms that lawfully opt out.

For a consumer the Act does not give you statutory interest and fixed sums in the same way. Interest on a late payment from a consumer depends on your contract terms and on what is fair under the Consumer Rights Act 2015. Read that Act on legislation.gov.uk on 24 October 2026.

That Act says a term in a consumer contract must be prominent and balanced. It must not create an unfair burden. A term that charges interest to a consumer must meet that test. It must be brought to the customer before the work. The customer must have had a real choice. The rate must not punish out of proportion to your loss.

That means in practice:

* With a consumer in their own home name a clear due date and the annual rate in writing before the work starts.
* Keep the rate modest if you want it to hold. 2 to 5 percent is more likely to be seen as fair than 15 percent.
* Do not add the business fixed sums of £40 and £70 and £100 to a consumer invoice and present them as law. They are not.
* Do not start interest the day after you sent the invoice if your own terms give 14 days. Interest runs from when payment is due under the terms. A consumer must have had those terms.

A court can award interest of 8 percent under section 69 of the County Courts Act 1984 where the 1998 Act does not apply. Read make a court claim for money on gov.uk on 24 October 2026. That is the rate the court can give when you file a claim rather than a rate you add to the invoice before a claim.

If the sums are large or the relationship is disputed take advice.

## Writing it on the invoice

Say when payment is due as a date rather than a count of days. Name the annual rate you intend to use. For a business customer you can name the statutory formula. For a consumer name a contract rate they agreed.

Example for a business customer:

Payment due by 14 November 2026. Late payment interest runs under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998 at 8 percent above the Bank of England base rate a year plus fixed sums where they apply.

Example for a consumer in their own home:

Payment due by 14 November 2026. Interest on amounts not paid by that date is charged at 5 percent a year on the amount owed per day shown as a separate entry where it is charged.

Keep the rate on the invoice so you can point to it later. Keep a copy of the terms the customer accepted.

## When payment covers part only

Interest runs on the amount still owed. Not the original total. In the calculator on this site put the balance that is late. Not the headline invoice figure.

If the base rate moves while the debt is late split the debt by the dates of each change. Work each slice on the base rate that applied in that slice. Then add the slices together.

If you have more than one invoice to the same customer apply payments to the oldest first unless the customer tells you otherwise. Record which invoice each payment clears.

## What to do next

Put a due date on every invoice. Use a fixed annual rate in your written terms for consumer work. For business work know the statutory formula and the fixed sums. Use the calculator to show the maths before you send the letter.

Keep copies of every letter you send and proof that you sent it. Note the date interest started. Stop adding charges when the debt is disputed and deal with the dispute first.
